We left Mumbai at 5:14 a.m. — the city's worst-kept secret is that it empties at dawn, and the Mumbai-Pune highway becomes a quiet, crooked ribbon between hills. By the time we hit Mandwa Jetty the sun was the colour of soft turmeric.

You can drive the full distance to Alibaug, but I never recommend it. Take the boat. ₹150 for an hour-long crossing of the Arabian Sea, with the Gateway of India shrinking behind you and the Konkan coast tilting up out of the haze ahead. It re-orders something.

Alibaug itself is small. Three streets, an old bazaar, the colonial-era Beach Road, and a fort you can walk to at low tide. We were there for the slowness more than anything.

The first night was at Kashid — a long arc of grey-black sand 30 minutes south of Alibaug, where the noise of Mumbai gives way to crickets and the slow boom of surf. Stay at a small homestay (₹2,500 / night, includes meals — Konkani fish thali at dinner is non-negotiable).

The road south of Alibaug doesn't belong to anyone. The hills lean in. The sea sighs. You drive at 30 km/h and that feels fast. — Day 1 notes

If you're driving, fill up at the HP at Alibaug Junction. After Murud, fuel stations get scarce and unpredictable.

Day 2

Alibaug to Diveagar

62 km 1 h 45 min Monsoon expected

The Konkan coast doesn't reveal itself in straight lines. From Alibaug, the road takes you up into the Sahyadris, then down again to the sea, then up again — a slow accordion of hill and beach. We drove with the windows down. The air smelled of cashew flowers and wet stone.

Diveagar is a long beach with a temple at the end of it. The Suvarna Ganesh Temple is small and gold-leafed and barely posted on travel guides — local fishermen know it as the patron of safe returns.

Diveagar Beach

5 km of empty sand. Walk barefoot the entire stretch — that's the visit.

The night was at Velas — a fishing village famous for one season of the year. From mid-February to early April, Olive Ridley turtles hatch on Velas beach. The villagers run the conservation. They take you down at dawn, point at the sand, and hold their breath with you while the eggs crack open.

A turtle the size of a wallet pushes its way out of damp sand at 5:48 a.m. The villagers clap, very softly, like they don't want to startle it. — Velas, Day 2

Day 3

Velas → Ganpatipule

140 km 3 h 20 min Mango orchards en route

Day 3 is the longest drive of the trip — but also the prettiest. The Konkan coast bends inward here. You climb past Bankot Fort (a Maratha-era headland fort with views in three directions), pass through Guhagar (laterite-stone temples, palm-fringed beach), and roll into Ganpatipule by mid-afternoon.

The temple at Ganpatipule is a Swayambhu Ganesh — the deity is said to be self-manifested from the laterite hill that the temple is carved into. Locals walk around the entire hill barefoot, in a 1.5 km circumambulation called the pradakshina.

Bankot Fort

Headland fort with a 270° view. Best at golden hour. Climb takes 25 minutes.

🏰 Maratha-era Free 5–6 p.m. best
View on map

Stay the night at Ganpatipule — ideally at a homestay just back from the beach. The MTDC Resort is fine but generic; the village homestays serve solkadhi at dinner that you'll think about for weeks.

Day 4

Return via Ratnagiri

312 km back 6 h driving Stop for Alphonsos in Devgad if Apr–May

You can do this drive in a single push — Ganpatipule to Mumbai is a long, contemplative six hours along NH-66. Or split it across two days with a stop at Pawas (a small spiritual retreat with an active satsang tradition) and lunch at Ratnagiri's mango belt.

The Alphonsos here are the real ones — small, fragrant, the colour of a sunset that stayed too long. Buy a crate at the wholesale market. They'll ripen on the drive home.

Ratnagiri Mango Belt

Alphonso country. Stop at Devgad-Ratnagiri stretch. Apr–May only.

By 9 p.m. you're back in Mumbai. The traffic at Sion will remind you you were ever away. But the next morning you'll wake up tasting salt and Solkadhi, and you'll know exactly which week of next year you're going back.
